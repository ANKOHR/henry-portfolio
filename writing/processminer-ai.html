ProcessMiner AI

ProcessMiner AI is a deterministic, rule-based process-mining CLI. It reads CSV event logs, reconstructs observed activity transitions, measures stage durations, waits, and queue lengths, then ranks bottlenecks and automation targets with published fixed rules. No ML, no GPU, standard library only.

Problem

The problem

A business process can exist in an event log without being documented anywhere. Teams talk about "the order fulfilment process", but the actual sequence of activities, who performed them, and where cases stall lives only in timestamped records. Before proposing automation, you need evidence about what the process actually is, not a whiteboard diagram drawn from memory.

The naive approach is aggregate statistics: average duration per activity, counts per day. That tells you how long a stage ran, but it hides where time went between stages. Wait time (the gap between one activity's end and the next one's start) and queue length (cases that have arrived but not yet started) are the signals that point at bottlenecks, and they require ordering events per case and comparing timestamps, not just averaging columns.

A second trap is threshold-less exploration: a tool that surfaces "insights" without published rules produces findings you cannot reproduce or argue with. ProcessMiner uses fixed, published rules, so every flagged bottleneck traces back to an exact threshold and the rows that crossed it.

Design

Design

The pipeline is CSV, then validation and UTC parsing, then per-case ordering and graph discovery, then durations, waits, queue lengths, and transition counts, then threshold findings, then the scoring formula, then JSON, Markdown, or HTML output. Each stage is a named module: fixtures.py, miner.py, analysis.py, report.py, and cli.py compose them in that order.

fixtures.py writes two synthetic logs: order_fulfilment.csv with a planted bottleneck, and clean_control.csv with zero findings. Both use fixed timestamps and sequences, so regeneration is byte-identical. The clean control log is the negative control: it checks that the thresholds do not flag healthy data.

miner.py validates each row and derives the graph metrics. Per case it orders events, computes stage median durations as end minus start, computes wait as the nonnegative gap between the current activity's start and the previous activity's end, and infers queue lengths from timestamps: events for the same activity that have arrived but not yet started. Headers are case-sensitive (case_id, activity, start, end, actor). cli.py reports rejected rows by row number with named validation errors, and valid rows still contribute even when other rows fail.

analysis.py applies the bottleneck thresholds and the automation scoring formula. report.py renders stable Markdown or HTML. The key data structures are the per-case ordered event list, the observed transition graph with duration data, and the findings list carrying the threshold each finding crossed.

Engineering decisions

Engineering decisions

Everything is deterministic by construction. There is no random number generator anywhere in the pipeline, fixtures use fixed timestamps and sequences, and no current timestamps or source paths are recorded in outputs, so the same CSV always produces byte-identical output. Determinism is what makes a finding checkable: rerun the tool on the same log and you must get the same flags.

Rejection is per-row, not per-file. Empty values, bad timestamps, missing timezones, and end-before-start rows are rejected with row-specific named errors, and the valid rows still contribute to the analysis. This mirrors real log hygiene work, where you fix or exclude bad rows rather than throwing the whole log away.

Timing and queue evidence stay separate signals instead of collapsing into one score. A stage can be slow (high median duration) without being a bottleneck, and a queue can grow even when each stage is fast. A bottleneck flags at median wait of at least 10 minutes or mean queue of at least 2 cases, and the impact score combines the two as max(median_wait/10, mean_queue/2). Keeping the signals distinct means a flag can be traced to its cause.

The automation score is a fixed weighted formula: 0.35 times repetition plus 0.25 times manual actor share plus 0.20 times duration factor plus 0.20 times rule suitability, where rule suitability draws on exactly five fixed suitability terms: payment_validation, shipping_label_creation, invoice_data_entry, address_validation, and email_notification. Published weights and a closed term list mean any ranking can be hand-checked against the inputs.

What the tests verify

What the tests verify

The suite has 23 tests across five files: test_miner (7), test_analysis (5), test_cli (5), test_fixtures (3), and test_report (3). They verify fixture determinism through byte-identical regeneration, ordered graph edges, hand-computed stage medians, queue-derived bottlenecks, zero flags on the control log, target ranking and score inputs, named row errors for malformed rows, stable reports, and end-to-end CLI commands.

The hand-computed checks are the load-bearing ones: stage medians and bottleneck flags are computed independently in the tests and compared against the implementation, so the suite verifies the published rules rather than just asserting that code runs. The control fixture asserts zero findings, which guards against thresholds that flag healthy data.

Scope and evidence

Scope

What it is not: the bundled logs are synthetic and fictional. Outputs are evidence about the fixtures only, never about a real company or its employees. It is a CPU-only, rule-based demonstration, not a learned model and not a production process-mining product. The boundary is carried in the CLI help, the graph JSON, the findings, and the reports themselves.

What the evidence supports: given a CSV with the documented headers, the tool deterministically reconstructs the observed transition graph, measures waits and queues with named rules, flags bottlenecks at published thresholds, and ranks automation targets with a fixed formula. The 23 tests verify those mechanics on fixture data, including the negative control.
